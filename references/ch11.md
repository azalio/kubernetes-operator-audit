# Chapter 11 — Case Study for Core Operator – Etcd Operator
Source: PDF 296–313; printed 275–292. Entire chapter read.

## Essence
Core-component automation has a larger failure impact than ordinary application management. RBAC Manager, the OpenShift scheduler operator and the archived CoreOS etcd operator illustrate simplifying sensitive configuration, coordinating component changes, preserving data and exposing recovery status. The lesson is careful control of supported operations and recovery assumptions—not adoption of an obsolete operator or automatic reconstruction of a failed database.

Checks are book-derived safety principles; concrete fault tests and modern recovery constraints below are explicitly audit extrapolations. No recovery commands are supplied or executed.

## B11-01 — Identify critical dependencies and recovery authority
Source: pp. 276–283. Applies: operators controlling authorization, scheduling, storage or other critical infrastructure.
Evidence: dependency graph, deployment location, blast-radius analysis, escalation and recovery ownership.
Fail: the operator is treated as an ordinary workload despite its ability to disable the platform, or its own recovery requires an unavailable operand/API with no alternative path.
Remedy: document failure domains and a supported break-glass path. Explicit bootstrap/API-unavailable analysis is extrapolation from the core-component examples; the chapter does not solve this dependency cycle.

## B11-02 — Safe and bounded configuration changes
Source: pp. 277–282, 288–290. Applies: powerful configuration or authorization controllers.
Evidence: allowed profiles/validation, generated RBAC or scheduling configuration, rollout sequence, invalid-input tests.
Fail: accepted input can trivially remove required access or render critical components unusable, with no guard or recoverable rollout.
Remedy: constrain unsupported combinations, provide tested presets where useful, and document consequences of low-level overrides. Do not require the historical preset or Boolean API shape.

## B11-03 — Backup success includes recoverability
Source: pp. 283–287. Applies: promised backup/restore features and critical stateful operands.
Evidence: backup and restore APIs/controllers, destination and credential references, operation status, a verified restore exercise against disposable data.
Fail: creating a backup request is called successful protection, stored backup is inaccessible/corrupt, or restoration silently targets the wrong cluster.
Remedy: validate artifacts and destination identity, report progress/errors, and test documented restore procedures. Integrity, retention, credential access and explicit recovery objectives are added audit tests, not details supplied by this chapter.

## B11-04 — Ambiguous failure does not authorize data destruction
Source: pp. 285–287. Applies: automatic recovery or stateful reconciliation.
Evidence: persisted initialization/recovery state, membership and health observations, restart/recovery branches, partition and lost-Pod simulations.
Fail: empty Pod lists, temporary API failures or operator restart are treated as proof that data should be reinitialized or restored over existing data.
Remedy: reconstruct durable state, discriminate initialization from failure, and stop/escalate when safe recovery cannot be established. This strengthens the chapter's conservative intent; do not copy its opinionated no-Pods restore heuristic as a universal algorithm.

## B11-05 — Quorum and failure-domain protection
Source: pp. 287, 289–290. Applies: quorum-based replicated operands.
Evidence: membership/health logic, placement and disruption settings, scaling/replacement sequence, node-loss tests.
Fail: replica count substitutes for healthy voting membership, correlated placement removes quorum on one failure, or scaling disrupts too many members together.
Remedy: respect the operand's membership protocol and distribute replicas across appropriate failure domains; verify available capacity. Detailed quorum-safe sequencing and disruption tests are extrapolations from the book's quorum/anti-affinity discussion.

## B11-06 — Deletion and uninstall preserve intended data
Source: p. 289. Applies: stateful/critical operators and cleanup workflows.
Evidence: owner references, finalizers, uninstall scripts, CR/PVC/external-data retention policy and destructive-action tests.
Fail: deleting the operator unexpectedly removes live clusters/backups or cleanup cannot complete safely.
Remedy: separate removing management from deleting the managed data, document retention, and require explicit destructive intent. Finalizer/PVC tests are audit extrapolation; they are not implemented in the chapter.

## B11-07 — Upgrade compatibility and recovery are operand-specific
Source: pp. 290–291. Applies: critical component upgrades.
Evidence: Kubernetes/operator/operand compatibility matrix, supported upgrade edges, rolling health gates, pre-upgrade backups and tested recovery procedure.
Fail: unsupported version jumps proceed, multiple members become unavailable without safety checks, or reverting an image is assumed to undo data-format changes.
Remedy: enforce the operand's supported path, validate each stage and halt safely on degradation; define realistic restoration/rollback limits. The chapter's one-minor etcd example is historical policy to verify for actual versions, not a universal version rule.

## Critical cautions
The book explicitly says CoreOS etcd-operator is archived; it is a historical case study, not an installation recommendation. Do not infer that running it manages or can recover the hosting Kubernetes control-plane datastore. Application etcd clusters and the host's datastore have different ownership and bootstrap constraints. CRD existence alone is not reliable proof of an individual cluster's prior initialization. Automation does not eliminate human error, validate a backup by itself, or guarantee zero downtime. Scheduler operators shown are distribution-specific. Any real etcd recovery requires current vendor/platform procedures, preserved evidence, authorized maintenance scope and verified backups; this chapter is insufficient as a runbook.
